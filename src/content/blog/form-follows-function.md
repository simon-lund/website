Of all the mantras, punchlines and quotes I’ve picked up, “form follows function” is the one that stuck.

It’s simple, and it’s hard to argue with.

Form. Follows. Function.

In other words, don’t put a spoiler on a toaster because it looks cool.

A toaster’s form should follow its function, and only its function. To toast bread.

The architect Louis Sullivan coined the phrase in 1896. Dieter Rams turned it into a way of working at Braun, and Jony Ive perfected it and made it a key principle of Apple’s design language.

Rams summed up his approach in three words, *less, but better*, and in ten principles. Good design:

1. is innovative
2. makes a product useful
3. is aesthetic
4. makes a product understandable
5. is unobtrusive
6. is honest
7. is long-lasting
8. is thorough down to the last detail
9. is environmentally friendly
10. is as little design as possible

But the mantra has a dark side. Function doesn’t imply beauty. Taken literally, a design only has to work.

By that measure, Victorian design fails. Gargoyles, fancy lanterns, decorated fences: none of those forms simply follow function.

Or do they?

It depends on how you define function. If function only means doing the job: no. But doing the job was never the Victorians’ only goal. They wanted things to be beautiful. Beauty was part of the function.

Even Rams, who wanted as little design as possible, put *aesthetic* on his list, right after *useful*.

“Form follows function” is still a powerful rule. BUT you have to understand the function before you can follow it.
